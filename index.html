浮世

A fascination with Japanese art

Japanese art has always drawn me to its quiet beauty, thoughtful details and unique way of seeing the world. From traditional paintings and craftsmanship to contemporary expressions, there is something deeply captivating about the relationship between art, nature and everyday life.

This space is dedicated to exploring the things I love about Japanese art: discovering its stories, appreciating its aesthetics and finding inspiration in the details that often go unnoticed.

Chapter I · Forms I fell for · 1 of 6

Ukiyo-e

Pictures of the floating world, and the first ones I ever loved.

The first print I ever loved was on a postcard in a museum shop. I didn't know the word ukiyo-e. I only knew the rain looked as if it was falling on me.

Time to sit with this · 6 minutes

A little history

Ukiyo-e means "pictures of the floating world". In Edo, now Tokyo, the floating world meant the pleasures of the city: theatre, teahouses, fashion, the latest gossip. The word once carried a Buddhist sadness about how fleeting life is. In Edo it turned into an invitation to enjoy it.

Prints were how ordinary people carried those pleasures home. A single print took four pairs of hands: the publisher, the designer, the carver and the printer. Each colour needed its own carved woodblock. The artist's name is on it, but it was always a collaboration.

Nov. Rain all week in Nottingham. Fitting, for Hiroshige.

Ask: why is the sky in so many prints darker at the top?

What to look for

  • 一

    The line

    Strong but never fussy. One outline does the work of a whole shadow.

  • 二

    The blue

    After Prussian blue arrived, every sky and river could go deeper. Look for the gradient at the top edge.

  • 三

    The empty paper

    It is doing more work than it seems: snow, mist, a mountain's highlight.

  • 四

    The weather

    Rain, snow, wind, mist. Ukiyo-e artists painted air.

  • 五

    The subjects

    Kabuki actors, beautiful women, travellers, birds, festivals. The everyday, made worth framing.

What stays with me

It is a still image that refuses to be still. A wave is always about to break; a figure is always mid-step. I think that is why I keep looking. I am waiting for the moment to finish, and it never does.

I love how strong the lines are, and how little they say. I love that nature is never scenery in these prints. And I love knowing that Van Gogh, Monet and the designers I studied all fell for the same small sheets of paper.

墨絵

Chapter I · Forms I fell for · 2 of 6

Sumi-e

Only black ink and water, and somehow it holds a whole mountain.

I saw my first ink painting in a quiet room at the end of a long gallery. I almost walked past it. From the doorway it looked unfinished. From close up it looked like weather.

A little history

Ink painting came to Japan from China with Zen monks in the 14th century. For them, painting was close to meditation: grind the ink, empty the mind, make the stroke.

The materials are almost nothing. A stick of soot and glue, ground with water on a stone. A brush. Paper. From that one black, a painter finds every grey between darkness and mist.

Compare with Tōhaku later.

What to look for

  • 一

    The single stroke

    A bamboo leaf is one movement of the wrist. It cannot be corrected, only accepted.

  • 二

    The tones of black

    Wet, dry, thick, thin. Count how many greys come out of one ink.

  • 三

    The unpainted paper

    Mist, water, sky, distance. The white is not background; it is part of the picture.

  • 四

    What is not shown

    A path that disappears, a peak cut off by cloud. The painting trusts you to finish it.

The simplicity of ink painting reminds me that an artwork does not need to reveal everything. Sometimes, what remains unpainted creates the strongest impression.

書道

Chapter I · Forms I fell for · 3 of 6

Shodō

One breath, one stroke, no going back.

I can't read most of the characters I love. That used to bother me. Now I think it lets me see them first as shapes: a bird landing, a branch, a dancer's arm.

A little history

Shodō means "the way of writing". It came to Japan with Chinese characters, and grew its own softer forms when Japanese scripts developed. For centuries, a person's hand was read as a sign of their character.

A calligrapher works with four tools, often called the four treasures: brush, ink stick, inkstone and paper. Styles range from careful block script to a running cursive so free that it is closer to dance than to writing.

Tried this once at a workshop. My circle looked like a potato. I kept it.

What to look for

  • 一

    The speed

    Where the brush ran dry, it moved fast. Where the ink pools, it paused. You can read time in a stroke.

  • 二

    The pressure

    A line thickens and thins as the hand presses and lifts, like a breath.

  • 三

    The balance

    Not symmetry. Each character leans, and the space around it holds it up.

  • 四

    The one chance

    There is no erasing. A wobble stays. That is the honesty I love.

The ensō

The ensō is a circle drawn in one stroke, often by Zen monks. Some are closed, some left open. It can mean emptiness, wholeness, the moment itself. Every one is a little imperfect, and none of them could be drawn again.

Bài học nhỏ永 (ei, "eternity") holds the eight basic strokes of calligraphy. Students practise this one character for years.

What stays with me

A kanji is not only language. It is an image, a movement and a feeling, all left on paper in a few seconds. I find it comforting that something so disciplined still keeps the trace of a trembling hand.

日本画

Chapter I · Forms I fell for · 4 of 6

Nihonga

Crushed stone and shell, laid on silk, until the colour seems to glow from inside.

I thought it was a watercolour until I saw it under gallery light. The blue had a grain to it, like sand. Someone had painted with a mountain.

A little history

The word Nihonga, "Japanese-style painting", was born in the Meiji era, in the late 19th century, when oil painting arrived from the West. Artists and thinkers such as Okakura Kakuzō wanted a modern art that still spoke in Japanese materials and ways of seeing.

So Nihonga kept the old materials: mineral pigments ground from stones such as malachite and azurite, white made from crushed shells, gold leaf, all bound with animal glue on silk or washi. But the painters looked at light, air and space with new eyes.

Find out: how do they keep the stone dust from falling off? (Answer: animal glue, nikawa.)

What to look for

  • 一

    The material

    Mineral colours catch light differently as you move. A photograph never quite holds it.

  • 二

    The softness

    Some painters dropped the outline entirely and let colour melt into haze. Critics mocked it. Now it is loved.

  • 三

    The subjects

    Seasons, flowers, animals, women, mountains: old subjects, seen with modern calm.

  • 四

    The surface

    Look for the grain of silk and the gleam of shell white.

Nihonga feels different from Western painting to me. It does not try to trick the eye into depth. It lets the material be material: stone, shell, silk. I find that honest, and somehow more alive.

現代

Chapter I · Forms I fell for · 5 of 6

Contemporary Japanese Art

The old quiet, said in a new and sometimes loud voice.

I came to Japanese art through old prints, so contemporary work surprised me. It was louder, brighter, stranger. Then I noticed the same things underneath: repetition, emptiness, the passing of time.

A little history

After the war, Japanese artists faced two pulls at once: the art of New York and Paris, and the traditions of home. Many of them answered by mixing the two, and by using new materials: mirrors, thread, light, photography, anime.

Today Japanese artists are part of the global art world, and Japanese pop culture is part of everyday life everywhere. The question of what makes art "Japanese" keeps changing.

Queued for forty minutes to stand in a mirror room for forty seconds. Worth it.

What to look for

  • 一

    Repetition

    A dot, a flower, a thread, repeated until it becomes a world.

  • 二

    Material

    Ordinary things, used at a scale that changes how you feel in a room.

  • 三

    Time

    Long exposures, old seas, memory. Many of these works are about how time feels.

  • 四

    Pop and fine art

    Characters and cartoons sit next to gold screens. The line between high and low is blurred on purpose.

工芸

Chapter I · Forms I fell for · 6 of 6

Ceramics & Craft

Things made to be used, and loved for the marks that use leaves behind.

My favourite cup has a chip on the rim. I drink from the other side, and I have never once thought of replacing it. That cup is how I understood Japanese craft before I knew anything about it.

A little history

In Japan, craft was never far from art. In the tea ceremony of the 16th century, tea masters such as Sen no Rikyū prized simple, rough bowls over perfect imported ones. Raku ware, shaped by hand rather than on a wheel, was born in that world.

In the 1920s, the thinker Yanagi Sōetsu began the mingei movement, celebrating everyday objects made by unknown craftspeople. A good bowl, he believed, is beautiful because it is useful and made with care.

My cup, chipped on the rim. Still my favourite.

What to look for

  • 一

    The hand

    Finger marks in the clay, an uneven rim. Someone made this, and you can tell.

  • 二

    The glaze

    Drips, pools, crackles. The kiln adds its own decisions.

  • 三

    The repair

    Kintsugi mends a broken bowl with lacquer and gold. The crack becomes the most beautiful part.

  • 四

    The material

    Washi paper, lacquer, indigo cloth. Each carries the place it came from.

Scroll sideways now, the way a handscroll is opened: a little at a time.

Works I return to

The scroll ends here for now. I add to it when a work stops me in a museum corridor.

What stays with me

I feel closer to handmade, imperfect things than to anything made by machine. They carry the hands that made them and the hands that used them. Mass-produced things are perfect on the first day, and only get worse. These get better.

侘寂

Chapter II · 1 of 5

Wabi-sabi

Beauty in imperfection

My favourite cup has a chip on the rim. I drink from the other side.

The word

Wabi once meant the loneliness of living far from society. Over time it came to mean a quiet, rustic simplicity: finding richness in having little.

Sabi is the beauty that comes with age: rust, moss, faded colour, the patina on an old bowl.

Together they describe something hard to translate: a beauty that is imperfect, impermanent and incomplete, and more moving because of it. The idea grew with the tea ceremony, where tea masters chose rough, humble bowls over flawless ones.

Written at the kitchen table, tea gone cold.

Where I see it

In art

A raku tea bowl with a lopsided rim. A crackled glaze. A scroll faded by centuries of light.

In everyday life

An old wooden table, a stone step worn smooth, a wall where the plaster shows its layers.

What it means to me

I find myself drawn to things that carry traces of time. A faded surface, an uneven shape or a small imperfection can make an object feel more personal, more human and more beautiful.

The word

間. Written as a gate with the sun shining through it. Not emptiness, exactly. More like the interval: the pause between two notes, the step between two stones, the breath before you speak.

Mono no aware

The beauty of transience

Knowing a moment will pass is what makes me stay a little longer.

The word

Mono no aware can be translated as "the pathos of things": a gentle sadness, and a deep tenderness, at the passing of everything we love. In the 18th century, the scholar Motoori Norinaga used it to describe the heart of The Tale of Genji.

It is not despair. It is closer to the feeling at the end of a perfect summer evening, when you know it is ending and you are glad you were there.

Where I see it

Cherry blossoms that do not last

People in Japan gather under the trees each spring, knowing the petals will fall within days.

The passing of seasons

The 72 micro-seasons in the header of this website, each lasting about five days.

Memories and fleeting moments

An old photograph. The last day of a trip. The light at five o'clock in late autumn.

What it means to me

Perhaps we love cherry blossoms not in spite of their brief life, but because of it. Knowing a moment will pass is what makes me stay a little longer to look.

第二章 · Yūgen

幽玄

Chapter II · 4 of 5

Yūgen

Beauty beyond words

Some things become more beautiful when they are not fully revealed.

The word

Yūgen is hard to define, which is part of its meaning. It points to a beauty that is deep, mysterious and only half visible: a feeling larger than what you can see.

In Noh theatre, the actor and writer Zeami made it the highest goal of performance. Poets described it with images: watching the sun sink behind a hill covered in flowers, or wandering through a great forest without thinking of returning.

Where I see it

In art

A mountain dissolving into mist. A figure seen only as a shadow. A painting that leaves the edges unfinished.

In life

Fog on a morning walk. A temple bell heard from far away. A face lit by one candle.

We find beauty not in the thing itself but in the patterns of shadows, the light and the darkness, that one thing against another creates.

Jun'ichirō Tanizaki, In Praise of Shadows (1933), translated by Thomas J. Harper and Edward G. Seidensticker (1977)

What it means to me

Some things become more beautiful when they are not fully revealed. I love artworks that leave something behind for the imagination: a feeling, a mystery or a quiet question.

You stayed. You don't need to understand it. Just let it stay a little longer.

自然

Chapter II · 5 of 5

Nature

Finding beauty in nature

Nature is not a backdrop. In these paintings, it is the main character.

The word

In Japanese art, nature is rarely scenery behind people. Mountains, water, trees, clouds and stones are subjects in their own right, often painted with more feeling than the humans beside them.

Behind this is an old way of seeing: in Shinto belief, spirits live in mountains, rivers and trees. And gardens are designed to borrow the hills beyond their walls, so the line between made and found disappears.

Today's micro-season: [tên tiểu mùa tự động]

Where I see it

Nature in painting

Hokusai's Fuji, Hiroshige's rain, Tōhaku's pines, Higashiyama Kaii's blue forests.

Mountains, water, trees, clouds, stones

Each with its own moods, painted again and again across centuries.

Human and landscape

Tiny travellers under huge skies. The people are part of the landscape, not its owners.

Nature-inspired composition

An off-centre branch, a corner left empty, a diagonal river leading the eye.

What it means to me

Japanese art taught me to look at ordinary nature again: the moss between paving stones, the way rain changes the colour of a wall, a single leaf on a windowsill. None of it is rare. All of it is worth noticing.

第三章 · Hokusai

葛飾北斎

Chapter III · Traditional

Katsushika Hokusai

1760–1849 · Edo (Tokyo) · Painter and printmaker, ukiyo-e

The old man mad about painting.

Artistic style

Hokusai worked for about seventy years and changed his name around thirty times, as if each new name were a new beginning. He drew everything: waves, ghosts, insects, bridges, sumo wrestlers, people yawning. His landscapes are full of energy, his compositions daring, and his lines sure.

Artworks

The Great Wave off Kanagawa (c. 1831) · Fine Wind, Clear Morning / Red Fuji (c. 1830–32) · Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji · Hokusai Manga sketchbooks

Themes & ideas

Nature's power · Movement · Mount Fuji · Everyday life · The joy of drawing

My favourite work

Fine Wind, Clear Morning. Almost nothing but a mountain and a sky, and the mountain is blushing in the early sun. After all the drama of the wave, this one is calm, and I love him for being able to do both.

Why I admire him

In his seventies he wrote that nothing he had made before seventy was worth noticing, and that by a hundred and ten every dot and line would be alive. I admire anyone who stays a beginner for that long.

第三章 · Hiroshige

歌川広重

Chapter III · Traditional

Utagawa Hiroshige

1797–1858 · Edo (Tokyo) · Printmaker, landscape prints

The quietest traveller in Japanese art.

Artistic style

Hiroshige was the son of a fire warden, and took over his father's post young before devoting himself to art. Where Hokusai gives you the drama, Hiroshige gives you the weather: dusk, snow, a sudden shower, the moon behind a willow. His compositions often crop a branch or a bridge boldly in the foreground, which later inspired European painters.

Artworks

The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (1833–34) · One Hundred Famous Views of Edo (1856–58) · Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge and Atake (1857)

Themes & ideas

Weather · Seasons · Travel · Places · Atmosphere

My favourite work

Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge and Atake, 1857. He made it a year before he died. Thirty years later, Van Gogh painted his own copy in oils. I like to think of them as two people standing under the same rain, thirty years apart.

Why I admire him

He never shows the whole journey, only one moment of it. A traveller on a bridge, rain coming down. His landscapes feel peaceful, and I feel as if I am passing through, grateful to be allowed to.

Chapter III · Traditional

Kitagawa Utamaro

c. 1753–1806 · Edo (Tokyo) · Printmaker, bijin-ga (pictures of beautiful women)

He drew the moment before a person speaks.

Artistic style

Utamaro was the master of bijin-ga, pictures of beautiful women. He moved in close, framing women from the chest up in "large-head" portraits, and paid attention to the smallest things: the curve of a neck, a hand holding a letter, a sideways glance. His lines are delicate, and his women seem to have inner lives.

Artworks

Three Beauties of the Present Day (c. 1793) · Ten Studies in Female Physiognomy · Book of Insects (1788)

Themes & ideas

Portraiture · Women's inner lives · Fashion and beauty · Expression through line

My favourite work

Three Beauties of the Present Day. Three faces that look almost alike until you stay long enough to tell them apart.

Why I admire him

The subtlety. Nothing is exaggerated, and still every figure feels like a particular person on a particular day.

Flat, bright, and not as happy as it looks.

Artistic style

Murakami trained in Nihonga before turning to contemporary art. His idea of "Superflat" links the flat surfaces of old Japanese painting with anime, manga and advertising. He also runs a studio and works with fashion brands, blurring fine art and commerce on purpose.

Why I admire him

He shows that tradition and pop culture are not opposites. They share more than we think.

No. 011 · Notes · Sep 2026 · Time to sit with this · 2 minutes

The beauty of things left unfinished

Why I find myself drawn to imperfections, worn textures and objects that carry the traces of time.

I keep a drawer of things I have not finished: half a sketch, a letter I didn't send, a book with a bus ticket marking page 112. I used to feel guilty about that drawer. Now I think of it as the most honest part of my room.

Not a record of things completed, but a place for things still becoming.

In Japanese art I keep meeting the same idea. An ink painting leaves the mountain half in mist. A calligrapher's circle stays open. A tea bowl is not quite round. Nothing is forced to be complete, and somehow that makes room for me.

The finished things in my life are tidy, and I rarely look at them again. The unfinished ones I keep returning to. Maybe that is what an archive like this is: not a record of things completed, but a place for things still becoming.

A little about me

Hello, I'm Bao Ngoc Vu (Grace), someone who finds joy in art, thoughtful details and the beauty of everyday things.

My fascination with Japanese art comes from its quiet yet expressive nature. I love the way it captures emotions through simplicity, how it finds beauty in imperfection, and how even the smallest details can hold meaning.

I studied for an MA in Graphic Communication at Nottingham Trent University, where this website began as my graduation project. It has since grown into a space I hope to keep returning to.

Why I am drawn to Japanese art

What draws me to Japanese art is not only its visual beauty, but also the feeling behind it. I am fascinated by its appreciation of nature, simplicity, imperfection and quiet moments. There is something comforting about art that does not need to be loud to be meaningful.

I am drawn to its silence, and its restraint. To the way it accepts that everything passes. To the patience of craftspeople. To mountains and rain treated as main characters. To rooms with almost nothing in them. And to things left unfinished, which leave a little room for me.

A space for my appreciation

This website is a personal space created to collect and explore the Japanese art, aesthetics, artists and inspirations I love. It brings together things I have discovered, artworks that have caught my attention and thoughts that I wish to keep.

Developed as my Master's graduation project in the UK, this space reflects my personal appreciation for Japanese art and my desire to share its beauty with others.
